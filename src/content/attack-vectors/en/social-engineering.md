The technical security of a well-run system — good practice, risk mitigation, a sound split of privileges — is hard to break. That is why the link attacked most often is the human one: a person can be deceived or pressured with fear into making the mistake the technology would not allow.

## Phishing

This is the most common form of social engineering: fraudulent messages that try to get the user to act on a third party's behalf — open an attachment, click a link, hand over credentials.

## Defence

- Check links **before** clicking them: the real domain, not the visible text.
- Scan suspicious attachments and URLs with services such as VirusTotal.
- Read the whole message for things that do not fit: sender, tone, artificial urgency, mistakes.
- Distrust by default any communication demanding immediate action on credentials or payments.

Social engineering does not exploit code: it exploits trust. The countermeasure is procedure and scepticism, not software.
